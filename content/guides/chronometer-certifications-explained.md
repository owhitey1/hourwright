"Chronometer" is a protected term in Swiss watchmaking. It means a movement has passed an independent accuracy test. It is easily confused with "chronograph", which simply means a watch with a stopwatch function.

## COSC

The Contrôle Officiel Suisse des Chronomètres is the Swiss institute that tests movements for chronometer status. Each mechanical movement is tested uncased over 15 days, in five positions and at three temperatures. To pass, its average daily rate must stay within −4 and +6 seconds.

A watch described as a "certified chronometer" or "officially certified chronometer" has a COSC-tested movement.

## Master Chronometer

Master Chronometer is a further certification overseen by METAS, the Swiss Federal Institute of Metrology. The movement must first pass COSC. The finished, cased watch is then tested again, including exposure to a magnetic field of 15,000 gauss, and must run within 0 and +5 seconds a day.

Because it tests the complete watch rather than the bare movement, it is closer to what you experience on the wrist.

## Brand standards

Several makers apply in-house standards that go beyond COSC and give them their own names. These are tested by the brand rather than by an independent body. They can be demanding, but they are not directly comparable with one another.

## Does it matter?

Certification tells you the movement was regulated carefully and tested individually. It is a mark of quality control. It does not guarantee the watch will stay within those figures for ever, since accuracy drifts with age, knocks and magnetism until the next service.

When comparing two otherwise similar watches, a chronometer certificate is a fair reason to prefer one. It should rarely be the only reason.

## Magnetism in daily life

Magnets in phone cases, bag clasps, speakers and laptops are a common cause of a mechanical watch suddenly running fast. A watchmaker can demagnetise a watch in minutes. Watches built to resist strong magnetic fields avoid the problem.
